WBase: how it works and why

Antonio Candiello · 5 November 2026

  • wbase
  • irrep
  • irreducible representations
  • Dynkin
  • Lie algebras
  • Cartan-Weyl basis

WBase is a C package of fewer than 1000 lines that computes the irreducible representations (irreps) of the simple Lie algebras and decomposes their tensor products. Given the highest weight of an irrep, it computes its dimension, index, weight system with multiplicities and Young tableau; given two highest weights, it finds the irreps contained in their product.

The article follows the order needed to understand WBase: theory first, then the algorithms, then the code. The numerical examples are rerun with V3.0, and where the original 1995 material contained errors the values are corrected (list).

What WBase is

The program uses Dynkin's approach, which works for all simple algebras, exceptional ones included: An, Bn, Cn, Dn, E6, E7, E8, F4 and G2. It was born for particle physics and supergravity, which need products of irreps larger than those in the classic tables, such as Slansky's.

Dynkin's approach

In Dynkin's approach a simple Lie algebra is described entirely by its Cartan matrix, an \(l \times l\) matrix where \(l\) is the rank. WBase contains no tables of algebras: it builds everything on the fly from this matrix.

In the Cartan–Weyl basis the generators split into \(l\) mutually commuting generators \(H_i\) and the remaining generators \(E_\alpha\):

\[ [H_i, H_j] = 0, \qquad [H_i, E_\alpha] = \alpha_i \, E_\alpha, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, l \]

Simple roots \(\alpha_1 \ldots \alpha_l\)\(l\) roots from which all the others are integer linear combinations, with coefficients all \(\ge 0\) or all \(\le 0\)rows of cart
Positive rootsThe roots with all coefficients \(\ge 0\); there are \((\dim G - l)/2\) of thempos, generated by wptree

Metric \(G\)\(G_{ij} = (\omega_i, \omega_j)\), scalar product of the fundamental weights; it is the inverse of the Cartan matrix, symmetrizedmetr

The scalar product of two weights \(x\) and \(y\), written in the Dynkin basis, is \((x, y) = \sum_{ij} x_i G_{ij} y_j\). The special vector \(\delta = (1, 1, \ldots, 1)\), i.e. half the sum of the positive roots, appears in all the formulas.

An example: the irrep 15 of SU(3)

The irrep 15 of SU(3) (algebra A2) has highest weight \(\Lambda = (1\ 2)\) and a system of 12 distinct weights on 7 levels. Three weights have multiplicity 2, hence 12 + 3 = 15 states.

The level of a weight is the number of simple roots subtracted from \(\Lambda\) to reach it; the height of the irrep is the lowest level, here 6. This is the table produced by WBase with the command T:

The system is symmetric: the lowest weight (−2 −1) is the opposite of the highest weight of the conjugate \(\overline{15} = (2\ 1)\). The original 1995 material gave (−2 1) as the last weight, a typo.

How the weight system is built

The weight system is obtained from the highest weight by repeatedly subtracting the simple roots, i.e. the rows of the Cartan matrix. In A2 the simple roots are \(\alpha_1 = (2\ {-1})\) and \(\alpha_2 = ({-1}\ 2)\). The recursive function wtree applies this rule:

  1. Start from \(u = \Lambda\).
  2. For each component \(i\) with \(u_i > 0\), subtract \(\alpha_i\) once, twice, …, \(u_i\) times, giving the weights \(u - k\alpha_i\).
  3. Each new weight is saved at the parent's level plus \(k\) and the procedure restarts from there; a weight already present is not processed again.

The rule stops by itself: negative components generate no subtractions, so the recursion ends at the lowest weight. The weights are kept sorted by level, because the Freudenthal formula needs the higher levels to be computed first.

In the diagram each arrow to the left (solid) subtracts \(\alpha_1\) and each arrow to the right (dashed) subtracts \(\alpha_2\). The tree tells which weights exist, not how many times they occur: (0 −2) has two incoming arrows like (−1 0), but multiplicity 1 instead of 2. Multiplicities need the Freudenthal formula.

The formulas

WBase uses three classic formulas, all written with the scalar product of the metric \(G\), plus the height formula. Each formula corresponds to a function in the code.

Dimension: Weyl formula

Function weyl. The product runs over the positive roots \(\alpha\):

\[ \dim(\Lambda) = \prod_{\alpha > 0} \frac{(\Lambda + \delta, \alpha)}{(\delta, \alpha)} \]

The weight system is not needed: the highest weight and the positive roots are enough. That is why the dimension of a huge E8 irrep is computed instantly.

Multiplicity of a weight: Freudenthal recursion formula

Functions freud and bfreud. Start from \(\deg(\Lambda) = 1\) and go down level by level:

\[ \deg(w) = \frac{2 \sum_{\alpha > 0} \sum_{k > 0} \deg(w + k\alpha)\,(w + k\alpha, \alpha)}{\lVert \Lambda + \delta \rVert^2 - \lVert w + \delta \rVert^2} \]

The weights \(w + k\alpha\) lie higher than \(w\), so their multiplicities are already known when \(w\) is reached. This is the slowest part of the program: each \(\deg(w + k\alpha)\) needs a search in the weight list.

Second-order Dynkin index

Function casimir, printed as C=:

\[ C(\Lambda) = \frac{\dim(\Lambda)}{\dim(G)} \, (\Lambda, \Lambda + 2\delta) \]

With this normalization the fundamental of SU(N) has C = 1 and the adjoint of SU(3) has C = 6. The original 1995 material writes only \(\dim(\Lambda)/\dim(G)\), leaving out the factor \((\Lambda, \Lambda + 2\delta)\): the code, however, has always used it.

Height

Function wheight, printed as H=: \(H = \sum_i \mathrm{whigh}_i \Lambda_i\), where the coefficients whigh are fixed for each algebra. H is the level of the lowest weight, i.e. the number of levels of the weight system minus 1.

Tensor products

To decompose \(R_1 \otimes R_2\), WBase adds every weight of \(R_1\) to every weight of \(R_2\) and then extracts the irreps one at a time, starting from the highest. This is the function wpdisp, in these steps:

  1. wsyst builds the weight systems of \(R_1\) and \(R_2\), with multiplicities.
  2. bprod forms the set of all the sums \(w_1 + w_2\), counting multiplicities (\(\dim R_1 \times \dim R_2\) elements).
  3. While the set is not empty:
    1. whigher picks the weight of greatest height: it is the highest weight of a component;
    2. wsyst builds the weight system of that component;
    3. bremove subtracts it from the set, multiplicities included.

The fast mode (commands F and L) skips step 3. It lists only the distinct dominant weights of the product, so it gives no multiplicities and can include weights that are not the highest weight of any component.

A physical example: the baryon decuplet

Three quarks form a baryon according to the product \(3 \otimes 3 \otimes 3 = 10 \oplus 8 \oplus 8 \oplus 1\) of flavour SU(3). WBase computes it in two steps: \(3 \otimes 3 = 6 \oplus \overline{3}\), then \(6 \otimes 3 = 10 \oplus 8\) and \(\overline{3} \otimes 3 = 8 \oplus 1\).

The decuplet of the spin-3/2 baryons is the irrep 10 = (0 3), in which the three quarks are symmetrized. With the convention used by WBase, u = (0 1), d = (−1 0) and s = (1 −1), the weight of each state is the sum of the weights of its quarks:

4Δddu(−2 1)
4Ξ*dss(1 −2)
5Σ*dds(−1 −1)
6Δddd(−3 0)

All 10 weights have multiplicity 1. The original material gave dds the weight (1 −2), already assigned to dss: the correct value is (−1 −1).

A second example: the meson octet

A meson is made of a quark and an antiquark, according to the product \(3 \otimes \overline{3} = 8 \oplus 1\). With the command P WBase computes it as (0 1) ⊗ (1 0) = (1 1) ⊕ (0 0): the octet plus a singlet.

The octet of the pseudoscalar mesons (spin 0) is the irrep 8 = (1 1), the same one that serves as the adjoint representation of SU(3). The antiquarks have the opposite weights of the quarks, \(\bar{u} = (0\ {-1})\), \(\bar{d} = (1\ 0)\) and \(\bar{s} = ({-1}\ 1)\), and the weight of each meson is the sum of the weights of its quark and antiquark:

LevelMesonQuarksWeightMultiplicity
0\(\pi^+\)\(u\bar{d}\)(1 1)1
1\(K^+\)\(u\bar{s}\)(−1 2)1
1\(\bar{K}^0\)\(s\bar{d}\)(2 −1)1
2\(\pi^0\), \(\eta_8\)\(u\bar{u}\), \(d\bar{d}\), \(s\bar{s}\)(0 0)2
3\(K^0\)\(d\bar{s}\)(−2 1)1
3\(K^-\)\(s\bar{u}\)(1 −2)1
4\(\pi^-\)\(d\bar{u}\)(−1 −1)1

Unlike the decuplet, here the zero weight has multiplicity 2. The three pairs \(u\bar{u}\), \(d\bar{d}\) and \(s\bar{s}\) all have weight (0 0): two of their combinations belong to the octet (\(\pi^0\) and \(\eta_8\)), the third, symmetric one is the singlet \(\eta_1\). This accounts for the 9 = 8 + 1 states of the product. The observed mesons \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) are mixtures of \(\eta_8\) and \(\eta_1\).

Data structures

WBase allocates all its memory at run time, because the size of a weight system is not known in advance: it can range from a few bytes to hundreds of megabytes. There are three data types, defined in wbase.h:

TypeDefinitionUse
wvecttypedef char *wvect;A weight: an array of \(l\) bytes allocated with walloc and freed with wfree
wplacestruct { tdeg deg; tlevel level; char vect[1]; }A weight within a system, with multiplicity (unsigned short) and level (short). vect[1] is the “struct hack” of pre-1999 C: the vector actually has \(l\) bytes
wblockstruct tblock { struct tblock *next; char body[1]; }A block of 1000 places (bsize); the blocks form a linked list

The blocks are “shapeless”: body is just a sequence of bytes, to which the macro place(i, b) gives the shape of a wplace. Using char for the labels saves memory but limits them to the range from −128 to 127.

Procedures and iterators

The WBase user works with a few high-level procedures and three iterators, without ever touching the memory blocks. The signatures below are those of the V3.0 code.

Walking through a weight system

pstart/pnext hide the step from one block to the next:

wplace *p; pcurr pc;
for (p = pstart(base, &pc); p != NULL; p = pnext(&pc))
    wdisp(p->vect);   /* also p->deg and p->level */

Extracting the irreps of a product

wpstart/wpnext return at each step the complete weight system of one component:

wblock *b1 = wsyst(hw1), *b2 = wsyst(hw2), *buf, *b;
buf = bprod(b1, b2);
bsfree(b1); bsfree(b2);
for (b = wpstart(buf); b != NULL; b = wpnext(b, buf))
    bdisp(b);

Generating a table of irreps

wsequence returns at each call the next irrep in order of dimension, up to those with dimension strictly less than maxdim, and at the end frees its own list:

wblock *b = NULL; long long dim;
wvect hw = walloc();
while ((b = wsequence(hw, b, maxdim, &dim)) != NULL)
    wfdisp(hw);
wfree(hw);

Two nested wsequence loops produce a table of products: this is what the commands U, V and L do. Exact results need WFULL passed to wpdisp; with mod = 0 the decomposition is the fast, approximate one.

Two sessions with F4

Two sessions with the exceptional algebra F4 show WBase at work. V3.0 reproduces exactly the numbers of the original 1995 sessions. Start wbase.exe, type F4 as the algebra, then a command.

Sequence of irreps (command S, maxdim 2000)

The two irreps of dimension 1053 differ in the index C (648 and 810) and in the height H (38 and 44): the dimension alone is not enough to identify an irrep.

Sequence of products (command U, maxdim 100)

With maxdim 100 the irreps involved are the 26 and the 52, and WBase decomposes all their products:

In each row the sum of the dimensions equals the product of the dimensions, for example 26 × 52 = 1352 = 1053 + 273 + 26. Here 1053 is (1 0 0 1) and 1053′ is (2 0 0 0).
